# The people deciding what to build are starting to build it

*Draft for review — October 28, 2026*

For years, building a software product usually meant passing a problem through several hands. Someone talked to users and decided what mattered. Someone designed an experience. An engineer made it work. That division can produce excellent work, but every handoff costs time and can lose context.

AI building tools change how much of that journey one person can carry. A product person can move from a customer conversation to a working feature. A designer can refine the code behind an interaction. An operations leader can build the workflow their team will actually use. Some experienced engineers have worked this way for years; AI is widening the set of people who can.

I think this is an emerging category of work: **the AI-enabled builder**. This person helps identify a real problem, personally builds a working solution with AI, gets it into use, and stays responsible for whether it works. The interesting shift is not that everyone can now generate code. It is that the person making the judgment about a problem can also test that judgment in something real.

## What the role looks like

The clearest example is a product-focused builder. They still do product work: talking to users, deciding which problems matter, choosing what to leave out, and learning from outcomes. But building is central to the job, rather than something they always delegate. AI tools let them create usable software, agents, automations, or product changes directly. They may work with engineers for review, architecture, and production systems. They do not need to own everything alone to be a builder.

[Overjoy's Product Manager posting](https://jobs.overjoy.ai/40195) is unusually explicit. It asks one person to conduct customer discovery, prototype and ship small fixes with Claude Code, work with engineering on larger changes, and measure the result. The qualifications ask for comfort shipping with AI coding tools; prior PM experience is an extra point, and a software-engineering career is not listed as a requirement. That combination matters more than the title.

The same pattern can appear in other disciplines. [Babylist's Staff Product Designer (AI Builder)](https://job-boards.greenhouse.io/babylist/jobs/5831620004) owns a customer experience, runs research, builds prototypes, contributes frontend code, and follows outcomes. [Rillet's Business Operations — AI Builder](https://jobs.ashbyhq.com/rillet/f8d4f207-1e14-49fc-a0aa-a997a0c9bc2c) applies the loop to internal ways of working: frame a problem with a team, build AI-powered systems or processes, and drive adoption. These are different jobs, but the person doing the thinking also does meaningful building and remains close to the result.

This is not entirely new. A strong staff engineer on an internal platform may already identify developer friction, decide which capability would help, build it, and see whether teams use it. AI did not invent that kind of ownership. What is new is the possibility that people whose expertise lies in product, design, operations, or a domain can carry more of the same loop without first spending years as software engineers.

## What it isn't

An AI-fluent PM is not automatically a builder. If the job is mainly strategy, specifications, and coordinating an engineering team, an AI-generated prototype may help communication without changing who actually builds. Likewise, an engineer using AI to finish assigned tickets faster has gained a tool, but the job has not necessarily expanded from implementation into problem and outcome ownership. A customer solutions role can involve substantial building yet still be a different job if the work stops at bespoke delivery rather than learning that shapes a product or a continuing system.

The boundary is the **mandate**. Is this person expected to use AI to turn a problem they help define into something working that people use, and to learn from what happens next? One sentence about using Cursor is not enough. Nor is a title such as Product Builder. Read what the person actually owns and then read the qualifications. A role can fit the category while still requiring deep engineering experience; that should be clear to candidates rather than hidden behind a broad label.

## Why collect these jobs in one place

Today, these roles appear under Product Manager, Product Builder, AI Builder, Product Designer, Business Operations, Product Engineer, and other titles. A keyword search can find some of them. It cannot by itself explain why they belong together, which ones actually involve personal building, or which require a previous software-engineering career.

A useful home for this category should do both jobs: show carefully checked opportunities and develop a clear account of the work. It should let someone with product judgment and a record of building with AI recognize that the role they want exists—even when the employer has given it an unfamiliar title. It should also help employers describe the difference between wanting an AI-aware PM, a conventional engineer, and someone expected to own the full journey from problem to working solution.

The category will evolve. The invariant is not a tool, title, or background. It is a tighter loop between judgment, building, delivery, and learning.
